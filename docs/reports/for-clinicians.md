# Voice-Dynamics Detector: Notes for Clinical Users

**Audience:** clinicians (e.g. NHS-trained medics, mental health
practitioners, safeguarding leads) who may encounter this tool's
"🎙️ ACOUSTIC:" annotations attached to a transcript, in a patient-facing or
safeguarding context. UK spelling used throughout.

**Purpose of this note:** to set out, in plain terms, what the acoustic
tagging feature can and cannot support you in concluding, based on a
structured validation exercise against real acted speech. It is not a
technical manual (see `docs/reports/for-engineers.md` for that) or a
simplified explainer (see `docs/reports/for-general-public.md`); it sits
between the two, using accurate paralinguistic terminology while explaining
the underlying audio-engineering concepts in plain language. The real
generated transcripts and analysis files behind this validation are
committed at
[`docs/calibration_outputs/round1/`](../calibration_outputs/round1/README.md),
if you want to see the actual tool output rather than a summary of it.

## What the tool actually measures

The `🎙️ ACOUSTIC:` lines you see attached to a transcript are generated by
measuring two raw acoustic properties of the recorded voice signal,
per stretch of speech ("segment"):

- **Loudness (RMS)** — the physical amplitude of the audio signal, compared
  against the average loudness of the whole recording. This is reported as
  "raised voice", "quiet", "whisper", or "sub-vocal/murmured".
- **Pitch (fundamental frequency, f0)**, extracted with a standard pitch-
  tracking algorithm (`pyin`), and its variability within a segment. This
  drives the "shaky/unstable voice" and "rising/falling pitch arc" tags.
- **Word-level timing and transcription confidence** from the speech-to-text
  model (Whisper), used to flag possible stumbles or repeated words.

These are all **acoustic/paralinguistic measurements of prosody and
arousal-adjacent signal properties** — they are not measurements of
emotion, intention, or truthfulness. A rise in pitch variability or
loudness can accompany many different affective states (anger, fear,
excitement, physical exertion, even a bad microphone or noisy room), and
the tool has no way to disambiguate between these from acoustics alone. It
does not diagnose, and does not detect deception.

## Why we tested it against real speech, and what "before" looked like

Before this validation, the underlying thresholds (e.g. "pitch variability
above X counts as a shaky voice") had only been checked against flat,
computer-generated speech — never real emotional delivery. We tested the
tool against 10 short (10–30 second) clips of real acted dialogue sourced
from public-domain films (via archive.org — full source list and
timestamps in `docs/CALIBRATION.md`), deliberately spanning a range of
presentations: anger/shouting, fear rising to panic, sadness/grief-stricken
panic, joy, contempt, resignation/flat affect, a deceptive-sounding
controlled tone, a surprise/shock reaction, and two calm/neutral control
clips.

We compared what the tool tagged against an honest, independent judgement
of what was actually being expressed in each scene.

## What we found: reliability by tag type

**"Shaky/unstable voice" — originally unreliable, now substantially
improved but still non-specific.**
In the original version, this tag fired on roughly half of *all* short
speech segments tested, calm and distressed alike — including on a
completely flat, unemotional line ("San Francisco last night") and on both
calm control clips. One of the two underlying measurements driving it (a
loudness-variability statistic) showed **no measurable difference between
calm and distressed speech** in our sample — it was firing on ordinary
word-stress patterns, not genuine vocal tremor. We have removed that
measurement as a stand-alone trigger; the tag is now driven primarily by
pitch-variability, which did show a real difference between groups (calm
recordings averaged roughly a third of the pitch variability seen in
angry, fearful, or contemptuous ones). After this change, all 9 false
positives seen in testing were eliminated, while every case that looked
genuinely correct on manual review was retained.
**Caution:** even the improved version should be read as "this segment had
statistically unusual pitch variability" — not as a marker of anxiety,
distress, or any specific clinical state. A confident, animated speaker
(as seen in one comedic-dialogue control) can also register here.

**"Raised voice" — under-detects shouting in short, uniformly loud
recordings.** This is a known, unresolved gap: the tool judges loudness
*relative to the average loudness of the same recording*, not against any
absolute standard. In our test, a scene of unambiguous shouting and
physical struggle **never** triggered the "raised voice" tag, because the
whole clip was loud throughout, leaving no quieter moment within that same
clip to contrast against. **Clinically, this means the absence of a
"raised voice" tag must never be read as evidence that a recording was
calm** — it may simply mean the whole recording was at one consistent
volume level, loud or otherwise. We investigated two possible fixes and
found neither could be justified with our evidence (see
`docs/CALIBRATION.md`); this remains an open limitation rather than
something silently patched.

**"Rising/falling pitch arc" — improved but not perfect.** This flags
segments where pitch trends up or down markedly across a stretch of speech.
The original version was too sensitive, firing on ordinary conversational
speech simply because it can naturally cover a few sentences with routine
pitch rises and falls. We tightened this and eliminated most such false
positives, but one borderline case remains on a calm control clip. Treat
any single instance of this tag as weak, non-specific evidence at best.

**Word "stumbles"/repetitions** — evidence so far is limited (only two
clearly-triggering instances across our entire test set), and in old or
fast-paced recordings, this tag may reflect the speech-to-text engine's own
transcription difficulty rather than the speaker actually stumbling. We
have not yet gathered enough evidence to fully validate or rule out this
tag's reliability under those conditions.

**No dedicated "surprise" signature.** The tool currently produces no
positive tag at all for a genuine, sudden shock/surprise reaction in our
test clip. This is a coverage gap, not a wrong answer — it simply has
nothing to say about that clip.

## What this means for clinical use

- Treat every `🎙️ ACOUSTIC:` tag as a **prompt to listen to the original
  audio yourself**, not as a substitute for doing so, and never as
  standalone corroboration of a clinical impression, a safeguarding
  concern, or a credibility judgement.
- **Do not use this tool, in its current form, as a lie-detection or
  forensic-voice-stress aid.** It was never designed or validated for that
  purpose, and nothing in this validation exercise supports that use.
- A tag's **absence** is not evidence of calm, truthfulness, or absence of
  distress — see the "raised voice" limitation above in particular.
- The tool's outputs are best understood as a **triage aid**: a way of
  flagging segments worth a clinician's own attention in a long recording,
  reducing the time needed to find moments of potential acoustic interest,
  not a device that has independently assessed emotional state.
- Sample sizes behind these findings are small (10 short clips, most
  categories tested only once or twice), so these findings should be read
  as an initial safety/reliability check, not a definitive validation
  study. Further testing against a larger and more diverse set of
  recordings (including, ideally, real clinical audio rather than acted
  film dialogue) would be needed before any higher-stakes reliance on this
  feature.

Full technical detail, exact thresholds, and the complete per-clip
evidence table are in [`docs/CALIBRATION.md`](../CALIBRATION.md) in this
repository, including citations to every source recording used
(archive.org).
